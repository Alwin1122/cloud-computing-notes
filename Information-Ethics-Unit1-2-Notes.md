# UNIT 1 — Ethical Theories and Concepts

## 1. From Wiener to Information Ethics

Computing ethics did not start with smartphones. It grew as machines began to handle information and make decisions.

**Norbert Wiener (late 1940s–50s), Father of Cybernetics.** He saw that automated machines would raise new social questions: Who is responsible when a machine errs? How do we protect personal information? Will automation take jobs? How do we use technology for society’s good?

**Walter Maner (mid-1970s)** coined **Computer Ethics**: the study of ethical problems that computers **aggravate, transform, or create**. Early examples: software piracy, hacking, unauthorized access, privacy violations.

**Shift to Information Ethics:** Today information also flows through phones, cloud, social media, AI, IoT, and big data. The real issue is not the device; it is the **information** being collected, stored, shared, and analyzed.

**Information Ethics (IE)** studies ethics across the whole **life cycle of information**: collection, storage, processing, sharing, protection, deletion. Typical issues: privacy, data ownership, IP, digital surveillance, AI bias, responsible use.

**How problems evolved**

| Era | Typical issues |
|---|---|
| 1970s–80s (PCs) | **PAPA**: Privacy, Accuracy, Property, Access; viruses |
| 1990s (Internet) | **Triple A**: Availability, Accessibility, Accuracy; piracy; digital divide; infoglut; hacking; speech vs censorship; porn; surveillance |
| 2000s (Infosphere) | Security, secrecy, propaganda, identity theft; plus military, health, political, religious angles |

---

## 2. Ethics, Morals, Values, Integrity

**Ethics** = rules from an **external** source (workplace code, profession, religion). People can face clashes (loyalty to a friend vs honesty in an exam) or new situations that old rules do not cover.

**Engineering ethics** = moral issues facing engineers and firms. Not only “can we build it?” but “should we?”

**Engineer duties:** public safety, honest reports, confidentiality, respect IP, no corruption, protect the environment, use tech responsibly.

**Engineer rights:** safe workplace, refuse illegal/unethical work, report unsafe practices without revenge, get credit for work.

**Morals** = a person’s own sense of right and wrong, shaped by family, education, religion, culture, experience.

**Morality** = putting those beliefs into **action**. Example: friend shares exam answers on WhatsApp — join, ignore, or refuse?

**Human values** = shared principles that make us decent (honesty, respect, kindness, responsibility, compassion, integrity, fairness). Personal values are the beliefs you treat as especially important; they drive behavior.

**How values evolve:** (1) society’s norms and culture, (2) your own judgment as you grow, (3) parents/teachers/gurus, (4) leaders, governments, and laws.

| | Morals | Ethics |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Personal | External (org, profession, society) |
| Same for everyone? | No — person and culture differ | Yes, inside that profession/org |
| Question | “What do I believe is right?” | “What am I expected to do?” |
| Style | Flexible | Formal and consistent |

**Integrity** = thought, word, and deed match. You tell facts so others can decide well, take responsibility, and earn self-respect.

---

## 3. What Information Ethics Is

IE is about how **people** behave when they handle information — not only about gadgets. It studies the relationship between information and people (create, organize, share, use) and the moral rules that should govern that.

It covers informational privacy, environmental effects of info systems, ownership/copyright, digital divide, and digital rights.

Examples: Should a student share the college Wi-Fi password with outsiders? Should a scientist post the Ebola genome on the internet?

---

## 4. Ethics in the Business World

Unethical choices (bad loans, hidden losses, unsafe products) can sink firms, send executives to prison, and hurt the whole economy.

**For the company:** lost customers, money, lawsuits, ruined name, layoffs.  
**For society:** job loss, investor losses, broken trust, weaker economy.

Staff, shareholders, and regulators now watch accounting, disclosures, safety, and product quality. Firms also face **respondeat superior** (“let the master answer”): the employer can be liable for employee acts even if those acts broke company policy.

---

## 5. Corporate Social Responsibility

**CSR** = the firm takes responsibility for how it affects the environment, community, and employees. Customers trust it more; staff feel proud; reputation rises.

Each firm must choose whether CSR is a priority and set concrete goals (Cisco is a common lecture example of a firm that did this).

CSR can **raise** profits (healthier menu items that also win customers) or **cost** money in the short run (electric cars that need discounts because buyers are slow).

**Supply-chain sustainability** (part of CSR): make and deliver products so today’s needs do not wreck tomorrow’s. Covers fair labor, energy, human rights, community. Intel’s energy-saving investments (~$45M) later saved about $23M a year.

**Why CSR and good ethics matter**

1. **Community goodwill** — easier to hire; polluters lose sales and attract regulators
2. **Consistent operations** — stakeholders know what to expect (honesty, respect, diversity, fact-based decisions)
3. **Good business** — fewer recalls, loyal customers, lower staff turnover, better long-run profit
4. **Legal shield** — strong prevent-and-detect programs can reduce criminal liability
5. **Avoid bad press** — e.g. Google’s $22.5M FTC fine over Safari tracking cookies

---

## 6. Improving Corporate Ethics

A real ethics program makes staff see a culture of integrity (ERC: ~86% vs under 25% with almost no program). Less pressure to cheat, more reporting, less fear of revenge (cut hours, bad transfer, delayed promotion).

**Signs of a successful program:** people ask for ethics advice; they feel ready for risky situations; ethical behavior is rewarded; dirty success is **not** rewarded; staff feel good about the company.

**Six actions**

1. **Corporate Ethics / Compliance Officer** — senior person for (a) compliance with law and policy, (b) ethical culture, (c) go-to expert for questions. Role grew after 1980s defense scandals, 1990s white-collar preference for firms with programs, 2000s accounting scandals, 2008 mortgage scandals.
2. **Board sets the tone** — oversees management for all stakeholders. Failure example: News of the World phone hacking — board failed to stop it; legal and reputation disaster.
3. **Code of ethics** — written values and rules for directors, officers, staff: flag risk areas, how to handle issues, how to report, culture of honesty.
4. **Social audit** — yearly check: did we meet social/ethical goals? publish new goals to staff, investors, customers, government, community.
5. **Ethics training** — workshops, real cases, how to report misconduct.
6. **Ethics in appraisals** — rate fairness, multicultural work, accountability, honesty with suppliers/customers — not only sales numbers.

---

## 7. Ethical Work Environment

A workplace where people act honestly, can report problems, and are not punished for speaking up. They need a trusted person: ethics officer, compliance officer, HR, manager, or ethics committee.

---

## 8. Five-Step Ethical Decision Making

Ethical decision making = pick the best action after looking at values, law, policy, and impact on stakeholders. It is a **loop**, not a one-time event.

1. **Write a problem statement** — What do people see? Who is hit? How often? How bad?  
   Good: “We stock-out on 15% of orders and lose $300,000/month.”  
   Bad: “We have an inventory problem.”
2. **List alternatives** — brainstorm with stakeholders; do not kill ideas early.
3. **Evaluate and choose** — effectiveness, risk, cost, time, law, ethics, effects on people and environment. The choice must be legally and ethically defensible.
4. **Implement** — people resist change. A trusted person must explain why, what is wrong now, and what they gain. Use a simple transition plan.
5. **Evaluate results** — Did it work? Any side effects? If not, refine the problem and repeat.

Legal ≠ always ethical (and the reverse). Use this process when both clash.

---

## 9. Ethics in Information Technology

Moral rules for using computers, software, networks, data, and the internet — legally, honestly, fairly, and with respect for privacy.

IT lets firms store huge personal data. Speed and convenience often get more attention than values. Public-concern examples: illegal music/movie downloads, spam, hacked banks and identity theft, student plagiarism, cookies/spyware that track you.

---

## 10. Utilitarianism

**What it is:** An act is right or wrong **only by its consequences**. “Utility” = usefulness. Intentions do not decide the case.

**Rule:** Do what creates the **greatest net happiness** (happiness minus unhappiness) for the **greatest number**.

Public policy and cost–benefit analysis are utilitarian (e.g. a waste plant: jobs vs health risk).

### Intrinsic vs instrumental value

| Intrinsic | Instrumental |
|---|---|
| Valued for its own sake (an end) | Valued as a tool to get something else |
| Knowledge, art, happiness | Money, a degree (to get a job) |

Study for knowledge = intrinsic. Degree for a job = instrumental. Money for comfort = instrumental; happiness = intrinsic.

### Act vs rule utilitarianism

Both want max happiness; they decide differently.

| | Rule utilitarianism | Act utilitarianism |
|---|---|---|
| Question | What rule, if everyone followed it, would maximize long-run happiness? | Which action maximizes happiness **in this case**? |
| Rules | Strict; breaking a justified rule is wrong | Rules are only thumb-rules; break them if this case is better |
| Example | Always tell the truth / keep promises | Lie if this lie clearly produces more happiness |

### Critique (exam favorite)

Utilitarianism can **sacrifice a few** to make many happier — slavery “for the majority,” or the **transplant case** (kill one healthy person to save five). **Bernard Williams’s kidnap case:** terrorists say “shoot one friend or we kill all twenty.” Critics say this ignores **rights, justice, and dignity**. Good results alone are not enough.

## 11. Deontological Theory (Kant)

**Deon** = duty. An act is right or wrong by the **rule inside the act**, not by the outcome.

You act from **duty** (respect the other person), not from fear of getting caught or hope of reward. Returning a ₹10,000 wallet is right even if keeping it helps you. Do not leak customer data even if a friend asks.

| Utilitarianism | Deontology |
|---|---|
| Do what gives the best result | Do what is right, even if the result hurts you |
| Keep a promise only if consequences are better | Keep a promise because promising is a duty |

**Kant’s categorical imperative:** Act only on a rule you could will as a **universal law**. Lying to borrow money you will not repay cannot be universal — then nobody would believe promises.

Kant’s four simple ideas: equal dignity; only universalizable rules; do duty because it is right; apply rules fairly to all.

**Criticisms:** duties can clash with no clear winner; too absolute (few gray areas); old duties may not fit today; some rules cause misery; “rule worship”; too much rationality, too little human relationship.

---

## 12. Rights and Social Contract Theory

A **right** is a just claim. **Legal rights** come from law (fair trial). **Moral rights** come from ethics (due process at work). Purpose: let people act without unfair interference.

Rights theory does **not** ask “does this help the majority?” It asks “does this **respect this person’s rights**?” Freedom of speech is the usual example: you do not silence one person just because many dislike the speech.

- **Negative rights:** others must **not interfere** (do not block whistleblowing).
- **Positive rights:** others have a **duty to help** (a coworker helps someone report wrongdoing).

### Social contract

People give up some freedom and accept rules so everyone can live safely.

- **State of nature** — life before organized society
- **Social contract** — agreement among people, or between people and a ruler
- **Sovereign** — highest authority after the contract (king, government, or the people)

| Thinker | Human nature | State of nature | Sovereign | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hobbes** | Selfish; need control | “Nasty, brutish, and short” | Absolute monarch | Peace, but few rights except self-defense |
| **Locke** | Rational | Not chaos; natural rights: **life, liberty, property** | People elect government | Government enforces those rights; if it fails, people can replace it |
| **Rousseau** | Good, but civilization corrupts | Peaceful early life | The people (direct democracy) | Fair life via the **general will** |
| **Kant** | Selfish but can be rational | Fiction; states are in a state of nature with each other | Government limited by moral reason | Fair representation without everyone voting on everything |
| **Rawls** | Selfish; design a fair deal | Thought experiment | Modern democracy | **Original position** behind a **veil of ignorance**: you pick rules without knowing your future race, gender, wealth, or ability — so you choose fair rules for everyone |

## 13. Virtue Ethics

**Virtue** = strength of character, built by **habit**, involving both feeling and action. Aristotle: aim for **eudaimonia** (a flourishing life). Seek the **mean** between too little and too much.

| Deficiency | Virtue (mean) | Excess |
|---|---|---|
| Cowardice | Courage | Recklessness |
| Stinginess | Generosity | Waste |
| Self-loathing | Humility | Arrogance |
| Deceit | Honesty | Over-confession |

**Three layers:** individual (honesty, kindness); work (punctuality, teamwork); professional (integrity, confidentiality — engineers, doctors, lawyers).

Why it matters: other theories focus on single acts; virtue asks **what kind of person** you are becoming. Right acts should come from good motives.

**IT professional virtues:** honest with clients; courage to refuse illegal or unsafe pressure; protect customer data; report bugs honestly.

Story: a doctor cancels a family trip for emergency surgery. Ethical because of compassion and professional character — not fear of punishment.

## 14. Analogical Reasoning in Computer Ethics

An **analogy** compares a new thing to a familiar one (computer memory ≈ brain: both store, but they are not the same).

**Analogical reasoning:** if two cases are similar in morally relevant ways, treat them the same.

**Two questions for every new IT problem**

1. Is there a similar situation **without** computers?
2. Does technology **change** the moral issue?

If no → apply the old principle. If yes → new analysis (AI decisions, face recognition, mass data collection).

**Case 1:** Hacker copies files vs burglar steals paper files. Same unauthorized access. Difference: digital originals stay. Still wrong — owner lost privacy and control.

**Case 2:** Hacker only “looks around” vs someone walking in unlocked houses and looking in drawers. Nothing taken, but both are privacy and property violations.

**Pros:** familiar examples; reuse old principles (honesty, privacy, property).  
**Cons:** you may miss unique tech differences. Always list **similarities and differences**.

---

## 15. ACM Code of Ethics

The **ACM** (Association for Computing Machinery) code guides computing professionals and students.

**General moral imperatives:** contribute to society; avoid harm; be honest; be fair / do not discriminate; honor property (copyright, patent); credit IP; respect privacy; honor confidentiality.

**Professional duties:** high-quality work; keep skills current; know the law; accept review; evaluate systems and risks thoroughly; honor contracts; improve public understanding of computing; access systems only when authorized.

**Leadership duties:** state social responsibilities; design systems that improve working life; allow only proper use of resources; involve users in requirements and validate the system; protect user dignity; train people on what systems can and cannot do.

# UNIT 2 — Privacy and Intellectual Property

## 16. Privacy and the Law

Organizations collect personal data to hire, lend, market, and serve customers. The ethical job is to **balance** business use against the person’s rights.

**Right of privacy** = the right to be left alone. In U.S. teaching: **Fourth Amendment** — people are secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects unless a warrant is based on probable cause.

**Information privacy** (Roger Clarke) = **communications privacy** (talk without being monitored) + **data privacy** (control who uses your personal data).

**Five key issues:** data breaches, e-discovery, consumer profiling, workplace monitoring, advanced surveillance.

---

## 17. Data Breaches

A **data breach** is unauthorized access, theft, copy, or disclosure of confidential/personal data. Causes: hackers, sloppy security, employee error, insider misuse, crimeware, lost/stolen devices.

**Personal information at risk:** employee records, payments, medical files, any PII. Public government records are usually not “personal information” under breach laws.

**Why it matters:** breaches rose sharply as more data went online and onto phones. **Notification laws** (most U.S. states) force firms to tell victims so they can change passwords and watch for identity theft. HITECH: notify people whose **health** data leaked, generally within **60 days**.

**Typical costs:** lawyers, forensics, letters, call centers, credit monitoring — from thousands (100 records) to millions (10M+ records).

**Prevention (exam list)**

- **Data minimization** — collect only what you need; delete when done
- **Need-to-know** access
- Train staff (passwords, email, no workarounds)
- Patch antivirus; **two-factor authentication**
- Encrypt devices; backups; **remote wipe** if a laptop is stolen
- Watch odd activity; dispose of data safely

**High-value targets:** PHI (health), PII, card numbers, bank/payroll data.

**Key laws / standards (lecture):** HITECH/HIPAA (health notice + penalties up to $1.5M for identical violations); PCI DSS (anyone who stores/processes/transmits card data — outsourcing does **not** auto-comply); FISMA / NIST for U.S. federal systems; state codes (e.g. Texas duty to protect + notify).

**Famous breaches:** Yahoo (~3B accounts), Aadhaar (weak API, ~1.1B), Marriott/Starwood (~500M), Equifax (unpatched bug, 147M), Target (vendor malware, 110M).

After a suspected breach: stay calm, investigate (forensics), call lawyers, **document everything**.

---

## 18. Electronic Discovery

**E-discovery** = collect, prepare, review, and produce **ESI** (electronically stored information) for court.

Almost all business records are now digital (email, docs, chats). Courts treat them as evidence.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ESI | Any digital file that can be evidence |
| Custodian | Person who created/holds the data |
| Harvesting | Collecting the data |
| De-duplication | Drop duplicate files |
| Metadata | Data about data (author, created/modified time, To/From) |
| Spoliation | Destroying or altering evidence (on purpose or by accident) |
| Legal hold | Order: **do not delete** relevant ESI |
| Retention policy | How long to keep records before delete |

**EDRM (Electronic Discovery Reference Model)**

1. **Information governance** — manage data through its life
2. **Identification** — where is the relevant ESI?
3. **Preservation** — legal hold; no alteration
4. **Collection** — copy without changing the original
5. **Processing** — drop junk (DNISTing), de-dupe, keyword filter
6. **Review** — lawyers pick relevant / privileged items
7. **Analysis** — who knew what, when
8. **Production** — give ESI in a legal format
9. **Presentation** — show it in court

Court can issue a **document preservation order** if the other side might delete files. Fail to produce → **adverse inference** (judge assumes the missing files hurt you), fines, or lose the case (*Coleman v. Morgan Stanley*).

**Authenticity:** prove the file was not tampered with — access logs, encryption, PKI/digital signatures, limited access.

## 19. Consumer Profiling

Collecting and analyzing online behavior and purchase habits to target ads and products. Tools: **cookies**, tracking software. Ethical worry: data sold without consent.

**Profile pieces**

- **Demographics** — age, gender, income, culture, family stage (observable)
- **Psychographics** — values, lifestyle, personality, tastes (why they buy; harder to measure)
- **Geographics** — urban / suburban / rural

**Usage:** heavy users (often a small % of people buy most of the volume — build loyalty), medium/light (try to convert), non-users (new to the category vs never will buy).

**Value equation:** Value = Total benefits − Total costs. Buy if benefits win.

**Primary market** = most likely buyers (most of the ad budget). **Secondary** = occasional buyers.

Criticism: profiling can feel like surveillance; data may be sold or used in ways the person never agreed to. Lectures mention **Code of Fair Information Practices** and **OECD privacy guidelines** as responsible-use frameworks.

---

## 20. Workplace Monitoring

Watching employee activity for security, productivity, and policy. **Fourth Amendment does not limit private employers** in the U.S.; state laws often favor the employer. Privacy groups want stronger federal limits.

**Policy principles:** openness, consent, consultation, respect private spaces, **proportionality** (do not over-monitor).

| Overt | Covert |
|---|---|
| Visible; staff know (CCTV, guards) | Hidden; only with strong reason (fraud/theft) |
| | Illegal if it ignores privacy law or private spaces (toilets, etc.) |

**Methods:** phone tap, biometrics (fingerprint, iris, gait, voice), corporate watch of staff/customers, internet/cookies/history, CCTV, computer activity (email, apps). Covert computer tools like FBI’s Magic Lantern/CIPAV are for **law enforcement**, not everyday HR.

**Track inside:** badges, cameras, email/web, phones. **Outside:** GPS in cars, phone location.

**Pros:** productivity, safety, less theft/leaks, less legal risk (e.g. harassment evidence).  
**Cons:** distrust, cost, hard to protect real privacy; “always watched” feeling.

---

## 21. Advanced Surveillance Technology

**Camera / DAS:** NYPD + Microsoft **Domain Awareness System** — faster response to crime/terror. Risk: leaked recordings used for blackmail.

**Vehicle EDR:** “black box” that records seconds around a crash — for safer car design and court evidence.

**Video today:** analog → digital IP cameras; **video analytics** (detect people/objects). Hardware: cameras (fixed or PTZ), encoders (H.264 etc.), DVR/NVR, video management, storage, network.

A surveillance network needs **flexibility, scalability, redundancy**, standards, and **RAMS** (Reliability, Availability, Maintainability, Safety).

Uses: traffic, borders, factories, banks, airports, stadiums, forbidden-zone alarms, behavior analysis, crowd/traffic flow, night video, number-plate/face ID.

**Stalking apps:** secret phone spyware (location, calls, SMS, browsing). Installing without the owner’s consent is illegal in many countries. Only with informed consent (e.g. parental controls / company phones where law allows).

**Exam wrap:** identity theft is a fast-growing fraud; e-discovery is costly and can expose private data; sites collect visitor data; employers monitor to raise output and cut legal risk; more data = more efficiency **and** more privacy loss if used badly.

## 22. Intellectual Property

**IP** = works of the mind — ideas, designs, software, inventions, formulas, brands, art. The valuable part is often **not** a physical object.

Why firms care: IP is a competitive asset. Three main legal tools: **copyright, patent, trade secret**.

---

## 23. Copyrights

Gives the creator exclusive rights (for a limited time) to **distribute, display, perform, reproduce**, and make **derivative works**. U.S. basis: Constitution, promote science and useful arts.

**What can be copyrighted:** literature, music, film, software, art, architecture, etc. Must be in a recognized category and **original** (not copied).

**Idea vs expression:** you cannot copyright an *idea*; you can copyright the *expression* (the actual code, text, melody).

**Fair use** (use without permission in some cases) — weigh four factors:

1. Purpose and character of the use (teaching vs commercial)
2. Nature of the work
3. How much was used
4. Effect on the market value of the original

**Infringement:** taking a significant part without permission (copying thousands of lines of code).

**Interoperability:** copyright should not stop programs from working together (opening another word processor’s files) if you do not copy the source.

**Global / digital laws:** GATT → WTO; **TRIPS** (1994) sets minimum IP standards; **WIPO** (UN) + WIPO Copyright Treaty for electronic works; **DMCA** — civil/criminal penalties, **anti-circumvention** (do not spread tools that break copy-protection); **PRO-IP Act (2008)** stronger U.S. enforcement. Protection still varies by country.

---

## 24. Patents

A **patent** is a government grant (USPTO in the U.S.) that lets the inventor stop others from making, using, or selling the invention — including **independent** inventors, not only copiers.

File → USPTO searches **prior art** (what already exists) → often ~35 months.

**Four tests:** statutory class, **useful**, **novel**, **not obvious** to a skilled person. Cannot patent: abstract ideas, laws of nature, natural phenomena.

**Software patents** protect a new computer-implemented process. Search first. Firms often **cross-license**. Lawsuits cost $3–10 million.

**Defensive publishing:** publish the idea so it becomes prior art — cheap, fast, blocks others from patenting it (you do not get a monopoly).

**Patent troll:** buys patents, makes nothing, sues or licenses for money.

**Standard** = agreed technical rules so products interoperate. **Submarine patent** = hidden patent that “surfaces” after the standard is widely used.

---

## 25. Trade Secrets

Confidential business information with **economic value** because it is **not public**, took effort to create, and the firm **actually keeps it secret** (recipe, algorithm, process).

| vs patents/copyrights | Trade secret |
|---|---|
| Time limit | None (as long as it stays secret) |
| Filing | Not required, no fees |
| Independent discovery | Allowed — law does not stop someone who invents it alone |
| Court invalidation | Patents can be struck down; secrets just need secrecy |

**UTSA** — more uniform U.S. state rules. **Economic Espionage Act (1996)** — criminal theft of secrets (up to $10M and 15 years). Hardware/software can be secrets if protected.

**Employees are the biggest leak risk.** Tools: **NDA / nondisclosure**, teach that customer lists are confidential, exit interviews. **Noncompete** clauses (time-limited, state law varies) try to stop key staff joining rivals.

---

## 26. Key IP Issues

### Plagiarism

Passing off another’s words/ideas as yours. Many students think “if it is on the web it is free.” Detection tools compare papers to databases. Combat: teach citation, staged deadlines, warn about paper mills, use detectors.

### Reverse engineering

Take a product apart to see how it works, copy, or improve. For software: compiler (source → machine code); **decompiler** (machine → source-like code).

Courts often allow RE for **interoperability**. Licenses often **forbid** it. Ethical debate: useful compatibility vs stealing a costly design.

### Open-source code

Source is published so others can use, study, change, and redistribute **under the license**. Premise: many eyes find bugs faster; software adapts and stays reliable. **GNU GPL** is a famous license; **OSI** defines/promotes open-source licenses.

### Competitive intelligence vs industrial espionage

| Competitive intelligence | Industrial espionage |
|---|---|
| Legal public/open sources | Illegal / unauthorized (steal secrets) |
| Feeds strategy | Crime |

Without controls, “research” can slide into espionage.

### Trademark infringement

A **trademark** (name, logo, sound) marks the source of goods. Infringement = using a confusingly similar mark. **Nominative fair use** = you may name the real product to refer to it, without implying sponsorship.

### Cybersquatting

Registering a domain that contains a famous brand, hoping the owner will pay a high price.

**Defense:** register important variants (.com, .org, .info); **ICANN** manages TLDs and gives trademark owners a chance before new names open; **Anticybersquatting Consumer Protection Act** lets owners challenge squatters (including some foreign cases).

## 27. Ethics of IT Organizations

### Contingent workers

No long-term employment contract; join a project, then leave. Used when IT staffing swings.

**Employee leasing:** workers stay employed by a **leasing firm** (payroll, benefits); the **subscribing firm** uses them. Both may have legal duties (**coemployment**).

**When to use:** need rare skills you will not need again; avoid hire-then-fire; faster/cheaper project — but you may not grow in-house skill.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Lower benefits cost | Weak belonging |
| Easy to scale up/down | Lower long-term commitment |
| | High turnover |

### Outsourcing and offshore outsourcing

**Outsourcing** = long-term contract: another firm runs the data center, network, or help desk.

**Offshore** = that firm’s staff are in another country (often cheaper). IT work can be done anywhere.

**Success checks:** right tech skills, project manager who speaks your language, enough staff, good telecom, strong on-site supervisors.

### Whistle-blowing

Publicly exposing negligent, illegal, unethical, or dangerous acts. Usually an insider; sometimes an auditor. Often high personal risk. Some do it for ethics; a few for revenge or money.

**Ask first:** Do I really want this cost? Have I tried every internal path? Am I breaking loyalty? Will publicity actually fix the harm?

**What to report:** illegal acts; **unprocedural** acts (wrong process, maybe not illegal); unethical acts (abuse, discrimination).

**Protection:** anti-retaliation laws; U.S. **False Claims Act (Lincoln Law)** + **qui tam** (citizen sues for fraud against government funds). Example: Peter Buxtun ended the **Tuskegee syphilis experiment**.

**Sensible sequence:** judge seriousness → document → try inside the firm → escalate → weigh personal cost → get experienced help → act → live with outcomes.

**Downsides:** broken trust, career damage, can wreck the company, personal safety risk.

### Green computing

Design, make, use, and dispose of IT with less energy and less poison (lead, mercury, cadmium). Goal: less power, less pollution, less e-waste.

**EPEAT** ranks products Bronze / Silver / Gold on 51 environmental criteria. **EU RoHS** (2006) limits hazardous substances; asks for ~65% reusable/recyclable parts and safe end-of-life.

### ICT Industry Code of Conduct (EICC)

Five areas: **Labor** (dignity, human rights), **Health and Safety**, **Environment**, **Management System** (laws + this code + risk + improvement), **Ethics** (integrity, no bribes, disclose, protect IP, fair competition, protect whistleblowers’ identity).
