## 1. How to read a Unit 5 question

Each topic in this unit has the same shape. Use it even when the question only names the topic.

| Step | What to write | The five values |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Define the technology or the change in one sentence | — |
| 2 | Name a benefit and an example | — |
| 3 | Name the people affected: workers, users, customers, vendors, people excluded | stakeholders |
| 4 | Name the harm: job loss, bias, privacy, unsafe conditions, exclusion | risks |
| 5 | Say what the organization should do | fairness, privacy, safety, accountability, inclusion |

**Competing responsibilities** means two duties pull in different directions. A factory wants higher output from robots, and it also owes retraining to the people whose tasks those robots take. A platform wants engagement, and it also owes users a space free of harassment. Name both duties, then say which safeguard holds them together.

# Automation and employment

## 2. Automation and employment

**Automation** uses software, robots, or AI to perform tasks that people used to do by hand.

The deck’s examples:

| Example | What is automated |
|---|---|
| **Robotic Process Automation (RPA)** | Software bots do repetitive, rule-based office work, such as copying invoice details into a database |
| **AI chatbots** | Systems answer user questions from predefined rules or from an AI model |
| **Automated testing** | Tools run software test cases and report errors |
| **Smart manufacturing** | Connected machines and sensors watch production and run industrial processes |

**Advantages named in the lecture**

- **Productivity.** More work is finished in the same time.
- **Consistency.** The same rules are applied every time, so variation falls.
- **Faster services.** Reports and common queries are handled quickly.
- **Fewer repetitive tasks.** People can spend more time on creative, analytical, and complex work.

New roles can appear in **AI operations, data governance, cybersecurity, and system maintenance**. The ethical question is who benefits from the productivity, and who bears the cost of the change.

**Example.** A bank uses RPA to copy invoice fields into its accounts system. Clerks stop doing that copying. The bank finishes the day’s invoices faster and with fewer typing errors. The clerks need new work, or their hours and wages fall.

---

## 3. Ethical issues of automation

Five issues from the slides. A full answer names each one and says who is harmed.

**1. Job displacement, reduced hours, or wage pressure.** Workers whose tasks are automated can lose the job, lose hours, or be offered lower pay for what remains.

**2. Unequal impact.** Low-skilled workers, and groups with fewer chances to retrain, lose more. People who already have digital skills are more likely to move into the new roles.

**3. Workplace surveillance and loss of autonomy.** Algorithmic performance scoring watches how fast someone works and can rank them. The worker has less say over pace and method.

**4. Responsibility gaps.** When an automated system affects hiring, promotion, or dismissal, it can be unclear who answers for a wrong decision: the vendor, the manager, or “the system.”

**5. Blind trust.** Treating the automated output as always correct leads to errors, harm, and decisions nobody is willing to own.

**Example.** A warehouse scores pickers by a scanner that counts items per hour. A worker who helps a colleague, or who slows down to lift safely, looks less productive. The score can cut hours. No manager reviews the reason. That is surveillance, loss of autonomy, and a responsibility gap in one case.

---

## 4. Responsible automation

Organizations should introduce automation so that technical progress sits alongside **employee welfare, fairness, and accountability**.

| Measure | What it protects |
|---|---|
| Run an **impact assessment** and consult employees before a major change | Fairness; people who will live with the change are heard first |
| Offer **reskilling, upskilling, career counselling, and fair redeployment** | Inclusion; the cost of change is shared |
| Use **transparent criteria** for automated employment decisions, and allow **human review** | Accountability; a person can challenge a score |
| **Limit workplace monitoring**, and explain what data is collected and why | Privacy |
| **Share productivity gains** fairly, and provide an accessible **grievance** path | Fairness |

**Example.** Before the bank switches invoices to RPA, it lists which clerk tasks disappear, trains those clerks for exception-handling and customer cases, publishes the rule the bot follows, and keeps a person who can override a wrong entry. Gains in speed are partly used for that training.

# Workplace changes

## 5. Workplace changes

Digital tools change **where, when, and how** people work.

- **Remote and hybrid work** uses collaboration platforms, cloud services, and digital workflows.
- **Flexible work** lets employees fit schedules around personal responsibilities.
- Employers can use the same tools to **monitor activity** and **score performance**.
- **Platform and gig work** can offer flexibility while shifting income risk and benefit risk onto the worker. Sick pay, steady hours, and insurance may not come with the app.

**Stakeholders:** the employee, the manager, colleagues who depend on a reply, the platform that sets the rules, and people who cannot get the devices or the connection the job now assumes.

**Example.** A software team works three days at home and two in the office. Stand-up happens on a video call. The same tool logs when each person is “active.” A parent who blocks school-run time looks offline. A gig courier on a delivery app chooses hours, and also carries the risk of a quiet week with no wage.

---

## 6. Ethical issues of the new workplace

**1. Privacy.** Excessive monitoring, webcam tracking, or collection of personal data turns the home and the workday into a record.

**2. Digital exclusion.** Employees without devices, connectivity, accessible tools, or a quiet place to work cannot do the job on equal terms.

**3. Always-on expectation.** Remote and connected work can create the feeling that a message must be answered at any hour.

**4. Biased or thin performance measures.** Decisions about promotion, salary, and job security that rest on incomplete numbers (time online, message count) treat busy-looking activity as quality.

**5. Unequal access to advancement.** Remote staff can miss promotion, mentoring, and the informal networks that happen in the office.

**Example.** Two analysts do the same quality of work. One sits near the manager and is mentioned in corridor conversations. The other works from a shared house with a weak connection and is judged by hours shown as green on the chat app. The second person is disadvantaged twice: by the measure, and by being out of the informal network.

---

## 7. Good workplace practices

Policies should balance **productivity, dignity, and inclusion**.

- Publish clear rules on **working hours, availability, monitoring, and data retention**.
- Judge performance by **quality of work, output, and responsibilities**, alongside time spent online.
- Give people the **resources** they need to work safely and effectively: devices, access, and a workable setup.
- Keep **reasonable hour boundaries**, look after wellbeing, and hold regular team check-ins.
- **Review automated evaluation** on a schedule, and check that it is fair and accurate.

| Risk from section 6 | Practice that answers it |
|---|---|
| Webcam and activity logs | A written monitoring policy, and a limit on what is collected |
| No laptop or no quiet room | Equipment and a real place to work |
| Messages at midnight | Stated hours and a right to be offline |
| “Green dot” used as a performance score | Evaluation by the work itself |
| Remote staff missed for mentoring | Check-ins and a fair look at who is put forward |

# Globalization

## 8. Globalization

**Globalization** connects people, businesses, and countries through international trade, digital technologies, communication, and **cross-border data sharing**.

Examples in the deck: **software outsourcing**, **cloud services**, and **international IT teams**.

The ethical demand is practical. An organization that works across borders has to respect each country’s laws, protect personal data, keep labour practices fair, and respect cultural differences.

**Example.** A firm in one country sends customer records to a cloud region in a second country, and pays a vendor in a third country to staff the support desk overnight. Three legal systems, one data flow, and one group of workers now sit inside a single service.

---

## 9. Ethical issues of globalization

- **Different laws.** Privacy, labour, and intellectual-property rules are not the same in every country. A practice that is allowed in one place can be unlawful in another.
- **Labour harm.** Unfair wages, unsafe conditions, and thin benefits can be hidden behind a foreign contract.
- **Cross-border privacy.** Personal data that moves to another country can lose the protection people were promised at home.
- **Culture and access.** Ignoring local languages, cultures, and accessibility needs excludes the people the service claims to serve.
- **Unclear accountability.** When vendors and subcontractors sit in a chain, it is hard to say who must fix a breach, an injury, or a rights complaint.

**Example.** A hospital’s records are processed by an overseas vendor. The vendor’s subcontractor pays night-shift staff below a living wage and stores copies of patient files on an unsecured server. The hospital still owes the patients their privacy. The contract has to say who reports the incident and who pays for the harm.

---

## 10. Responsible global practice

| Practice | Duty it meets |
|---|---|
| **Due diligence** on vendors, subcontractors, and labour conditions | Fairness and safety for workers the public never sees |
| Strong **privacy and security** standards, plus a check of **cross-border transfer** rules | Privacy |
| Respect for **intellectual property, local law, cultural difference, and human rights** | Inclusion and legality |
| **Clear contracts**: who is responsible, audit rights, incident reporting | Accountability |
| **Accessible complaint channels** and a remedy for people the service harms | Accountability and fairness |

Due diligence means looking before you sign: wages, hours, safety, and where the data will actually sit. A contract that cannot be audited leaves the responsibility gap from the globalization slide open.

# The digital divide

## 11. The digital divide

The **digital divide** is inequality in access to digital technologies and in the ability to use them effectively.

Four types from the lecture:

| Type | What is unequal |
|---|---|
| **Access divide** | Devices and internet connectivity |
| **Skills divide** | Ability to use digital technologies effectively and safely |
| **Usage divide** | Ability to turn that use into educational, economic, and social benefit |
| **Accessibility divide** | Barriers from disability, language, or inaccessible design |

A person can own a phone and still be on the wrong side of the skills divide, the usage divide, or the accessibility divide. Owning a device is only the first row.

**Ethical issues**

- **Educational inequality**, when online learning is out of reach.
- **Economic inequality**, when jobs assume a connection and a practiced user.
- **Social exclusion** from online healthcare, banking, and government services.
- **Greater exposure to scams and misinformation**, when digital literacy is thin.

**Suitable measures**

- Affordable internet, devices, and **public access centres**.
- **Digital-literacy and cybersecurity training**.
- Services that are **accessible, mobile-friendly, and in local languages**.
- **Assisted and offline alternatives** for essential services, so a person who cannot get online can still reach healthcare, banking, and government.

**Example.** A college moves tutorials and assignment submission fully online. A student with a shared phone and no home broadband misses classes (access). A student who can connect, and cannot use the portal’s unlabeled buttons, also misses them (accessibility). A help desk in the library, a mobile page in the local language, and a paper submission route close both gaps.

The five values land here as **inclusion** first, then **safety** (literacy against scams) and **fairness** (essential services that still have an offline door).

# Artificial intelligence and ethics

## 12. Artificial intelligence and ethics

**Artificial intelligence (AI)** is technology that lets machines perform tasks that require human-like intelligence.

Applications named in the deck: **healthcare diagnosis, recruitment, recommendation systems, chatbots, and autonomous vehicles**.

**Ethical issues**

| Issue | What goes wrong |
|---|---|
| **Bias and discrimination** | The system produces unfair decisions, often because the training data repeats an old pattern |
| **Privacy** | Personal data is collected or misused |
| **Lack of transparency** | A decision is hard to explain to the person it affects |
| **Accountability** | When the decision harms someone, it is unclear who is responsible |
| **Job displacement** | Automation of intelligent tasks replaces certain jobs (this links back to sections 2–4) |

**Stakeholders** in a hiring system: the applicant, the recruiter, the vendor who trained the model, and the groups who were under-represented in the past hiring data. The benefit is speed. The risk is a rejection the applicant cannot understand and nobody will own.

**Example.** A hospital tool suggests a diagnosis from past records. If those records under-represent a community, the suggestion can be weaker for patients from that community. The clinician still has to be able to see why the suggestion appeared, and the hospital has to be able to say who answers if the suggestion is followed and the patient is harmed.

---

## 13. Responsible AI

The deck’s six practices map directly onto the learning outcomes.

| Practice | Value |
|---|---|
| Use **diverse, representative data** to reduce bias | Fairness |
| **Protect personal data** and obtain appropriate **consent** | Privacy |
| Keep decisions **transparent and explainable** | Accountability |
| Keep **human oversight** for important decisions | Safety and accountability |
| **Establish who is accountable** for errors and harm | Accountability |
| **Test regularly** for fairness, safety, and reliability | Safety |

Human oversight means a person can stop, override, or refuse the output when the decision affects health, hiring, credit, or physical safety. A test that runs once at launch does not meet the last row. The slide says the testing is regular.

**Example.** The recruitment model is trained on applications from more than one demographic group, applicants are told a model is used, a recruiter must review every rejection, and the firm rechecks error rates by group each quarter. If one group is rejected far more often for the same qualifications, the model is taken out of the decision until the cause is fixed.

# Virtual reality and ethics

## 14. Virtual reality and ethics

**Virtual reality (VR)** creates an immersive, computer-generated environment in which a user can interact with a simulated world.

Applications: **education, medical training, gaming, engineering design, and virtual meetings**. The slides also list simulation-based training, therapy, design, and social interaction.

Two characteristics matter for ethics.

- **Immersion.** Realistic interaction lets people practise in a controlled environment. A surgeon or a student can rehearse without the real-world stakes.
- **Rich data.** VR systems can collect **movement, gaze, voice, spatial, and interaction** data. That is more intimate than a normal web log, because it records the body and where the person looked.

**Ethical issues**

| Issue | What the slide says |
|---|---|
| **Privacy** | Devices can collect movement, voice, and behavioural data |
| **Psychological effects** | Heavy use can cause stress, discomfort, or confusion |
| **User safety** | Motion sickness and physical injury |
| **Consent** | Users can be exposed to unwanted interactions or content |
| **Accessibility** | Physical, financial, or technical barriers leave some people out |

**Example.** A medical college uses VR so students can practise a procedure. The headset records where each student looked and how their hands moved. That recording is useful for feedback. It is also a behavioural profile. Using it later to rank students, without a separate consent, breaks the privacy and consent rows.

---

## 15. Safe and ethical use of VR

- Collect **only the data that is necessary**, and protect it.
- Get **consent** before collecting or sharing personal information.
- Give **safety instructions, breaks, and age-appropriate content**.
- **Prevent harassment** and unwanted interactions inside the virtual space.
- Design **inclusive experiences** for users with different abilities.
- Provide **reporting and moderation**.

**Worked example from the lecture.** In a VR classroom, a student is harassed by another participant. The platform should provide **blocking, reporting, and moderation** so the room stays a safe place to learn. Safety instructions and breaks cover motion sickness and fatigue. Inclusive design covers students who cannot use the default controllers or who cannot afford a headset: the college still needs another way for them to complete the same training.

# Cyberspace and ethics

## 16. Cyberspace and ethics

**Cyberspace** is the digital environment created by interconnected computers, networks, websites, and online services.

Applications: **online education, e-commerce, social networking, digital banking, and communication**.

**Ethical principles named on the slide:** privacy, security, honesty, respect, intellectual property rights, and responsible communication.

**Ethical issues**

- Sharing personal information **without consent**.
- Spreading **misinformation and fake news**.
- **Plagiarism and copyright infringement**.
- **Hacking, identity theft, and online fraud**.
- Posting **abusive or harmful content**.

These five issues are the failures of the six principles. Sharing without consent fails privacy. Fake news fails honesty. Plagiarism fails intellectual property and honesty. Hacking and fraud fail security. Abusive posts fail respect and responsible communication.

**Example.** A student pastes a paid article into an assignment (copyright and plagiarism), then forwards a medical rumour in the class group without checking it (misinformation). Both acts happen in cyberspace, and both have a named duty on the next slide.

---

## 17. Responsible behaviour in cyberspace

| Behaviour | Principle it serves |
|---|---|
| Respect other people’s privacy, and get consent before sharing their information | Privacy, respect |
| **Verify** information before forwarding it | Honesty |
| Follow **copyright** law and **acknowledge** the original creator | Intellectual property |
| Use **strong passwords** and **multi-factor authentication** | Security |
| Stay out of systems you are not allowed to enter, and **report vulnerabilities responsibly** | Security, accountability |
| Communicate respectfully, and **report harmful activity** | Respect |

Responsible disclosure of a vulnerability means telling the party who can fix it, through a proper channel, so the flaw is closed. It is the opposite of using the flaw, or posting it in a way that invites immediate abuse.

# Cyberbullying

## 18. Cyberbullying

**Cyberbullying** is repeated or harmful behaviour carried out through digital platforms to **intimidate, humiliate, or threaten** another person.

| Type | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Harassment** | Repeated abusive messages |
| **Impersonation** | Fake accounts used to damage someone’s reputation |
| **Outing** | Sharing private information or images without consent |
| **Exclusion** | Deliberately leaving someone out of online groups |
| **Cyberstalking** | Repeated online monitoring or threatening contact |

**Effects named in the lecture:** anxiety, emotional distress, social isolation, reduced academic performance, and reputational harm.

A one-off rude comment and a campaign are different in degree. The definition stresses **repeated or harmful** conduct aimed at intimidation, humiliation, or threat. Outing is also a privacy failure. Impersonation is also a failure of honesty and of respect. Exclusion is a social harm even when no insult is typed.

**Example.** Classmates make a fake account in a student’s name, post embarrassing claims, and keep the student out of the project group chat. That is impersonation plus exclusion. The harm shows up as distress, a drop in coursework, and a reputation the student did not create.

---

## 19. Prevention and response to cyberbullying

What the person targeted, and everyone else, should do:

- Think before posting, and communicate respectfully.
- Do not like, forward, or encourage abusive content. Forwarding is how a small attack becomes a public one.
- **Save evidence**: screenshots, messages, and URLs.
- **Block** the offender and **report** the content to the platform.
- Tell a **trusted adult, teacher, institution, or appropriate authority**.
- **Protect personal information** and review privacy settings, so outing and impersonation are harder.

What schools and platforms should do:

- Publish **clear policies**.
- **Support the people affected**, alongside punishing or removing the content.

Evidence matters because a report without screenshots, dates, and addresses is easy to dismiss. Support matters because removing a post does not by itself repair anxiety, isolation, or a damaged reputation.

**Example.** A student receives repeated threats in a game chat and then sees a private photo in the class group. They screenshot the chat and the post, block the accounts, report both to the platform, and tell a teacher the same day. The school’s policy says who investigates, and the student is offered support while that happens. Classmates are told that forwarding the photo counts as taking part.

## 20. How to write the answers

Use the learning outcomes as the paragraph order. Definition first, then stakeholders and the benefit, then the risks, then safeguards tied to fairness, privacy, safety, accountability, and inclusion.

### If the question names one topic

| Question is about | Open with | Then list | Close with |
|---|---|---|---|
| Automation | Software, robots, or AI doing tasks people used to do; RPA, chatbots, testing, smart manufacturing | Five ethical issues | Impact assessment, reskilling, human review, limited monitoring, shared gains |
| Workplace change | Remote, hybrid, flexible, and gig work | Privacy, exclusion, always-on hours, thin metrics, missed mentoring | Written policies, judge the work, provide tools, hour boundaries, audit the scores |
| Globalization | Trade, digital systems, and cross-border data | Laws, labour, data transfer, culture, vendor chains | Due diligence, transfer rules, rights, contracts with audit and incident duties, a remedy |
| Digital divide | Inequality of access and of effective use | Four divides, then education, jobs, services, scams | Affordable access, literacy, accessible local-language design, offline alternatives |
| AI | Machines doing tasks that need human-like intelligence | Bias, privacy, opacity, accountability, job loss | Representative data, consent, explanation, human oversight, named responsibility, regular tests |
| VR | An immersive simulated world; body and gaze data | Privacy, psychological effects, motion sickness and injury, consent, access | Data minimization, consent, breaks and age limits, anti-harassment tools, inclusive design, the VR-classroom example |
| Cyberspace | The online environment of networks and services | Consent, misinformation, plagiarism, hacking and fraud, abuse | The six behaviours, especially verify, copyright, MFA, and responsible reporting |
| Cyberbullying | Repeated digital harm meant to intimidate, humiliate, or threaten | Five types and five effects | Evidence, block, report, tell an authority, school policy and support |

### If the question is broad (“new frontiers” or “emerging challenges”)

Write one sentence on the learning outcome, then one short block on each frontier. In every block, give the definition and one safeguard. If time allows, add one worked example from automation (the invoice bots or the warehouse score), the VR classroom, or the cyberbullying screenshots.
